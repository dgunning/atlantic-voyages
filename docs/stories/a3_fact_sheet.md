## The story in four beats

1. **One ship.** The *African Queen* of Bristol leaves Old Calabar and lands captives at
   Montego Bay in January 1793. Most of those whose sex was recorded were women.
2. **The pattern.** From 1763 to 1808, Montego Bay was Jamaica's second port of arrival. Most
   of the people landed there came from one coast, the Bight of Biafra (Igbo and Ibibio
   country), far more than at Kingston.
3. **The people who stayed.** Runaway notices in the Montego Bay newspaper show the same lean
   towards the Bight of Biafra among the African-born people of the north-west.
4. **The end, and after.** British abolition stopped the ships in 1807–1808. Twenty-three
   years later, the Christmas Rebellion began at Kensington estate in the hills above
   Montego Bay. The article should say plainly what the data cannot show: who took part, and
   where they were born.

## What the regions mean

SlaveVoyages' regions are named for the coast, not today's countries. Gloss them in the
article:

- **Bight of Biafra**: the Niger delta and Cross River (Bonny, New Calabar, Old Calabar;
  Igbo and Ibibio country, today south-eastern Nigeria). The region also takes in the
  Cameroon coast, the Gabon estuary and the Gulf of Guinea islands, but Montego Bay's ships
  loaded almost all at the Nigerian ports (F17).
- **West Central Africa**: the coast south of the equator, the Loango coast, Kongo and
  Angola (today southern Gabon, the Republic of Congo, Cabinda, the DR Congo coast and
  Angola). It does not include Cameroon.
- **Gold Coast**: today's Ghana. **Bight of Benin**: today's Togo, Benin and south-western
  Nigeria. **Upper Guinea**: Senegal to Côte d'Ivoire.

## Prior work (checked 2026-10-20)

- No published study found that compares African origins of people landed at Montego Bay
  and Kingston, or runaway origins by newspaper against landings by port. Searches saw
  mostly abstracts, so "not found" is not proof.
- Wood and Smith, "Fugitives from Slavery in Jamaica, 1718–1795" (the article with the
  dataset, https://jsdp.enslaved.org/assets/downloaded/40-59-23/FSJ-Article-Final-20220517.pdf):
  read in full; no results tables, and no origins by newspaper or town. It reports women and
  girls as about 20% of escapees (ours: 172 of 782, 22%), branding of at least 80%, and
  discusses "Eboe" and "Coromantee" as unstable terms.
- Still to read: Chambers, "Biafran African Runaways in 18th-Century Jamaica and
  Saint-Domingue", *Igbo Studies Review* 2 (2014): the one likely overlap.
- The north-west Igbo concentration is stated in secondary sources without figures; the
  article's new contribution is the numbers. Say "the figures show", not "historians have
  not noticed".

**Wording for the runaway figures (F15, F16, F19):** the origins are the terms enslavers
wrote in the notices ("Eboe", "Coromantee", "Congo"), matched to regions by Enslaved.org.
Wood and Smith note that a term like "Eboe" was sometimes a place and sometimes a cultural
label. Write "described as Eboe or Igbo" or "named with a term from the Bight of Biafra",
not "born in the Bight of Biafra".
