# Calabar: from a slaving port to a Jamaican school

How the name of a port in the Bight of Biafra became the name of a Baptist theological
college and a boys' high school in Jamaica, and how both are tied to the slave trade and
its abolition.

This is background for `notebooks/02_calabar_to_jamaica.py`. The figures in section 2 come
from that notebook, which checks them against the voyage data. Everything else comes from the
sources listed at the end. Points the sources do not settle are marked **unverified**.

## 1. Which Calabar

- **Old Calabar is today's Calabar**, in Cross River State, Nigeria. It sits on the Calabar
  River near the Cross River estuary. Its Efik name is Akwa Akpa, and its trading towns were
  Duke Town and Creek Town. Europeans called it "Old Calabar" to tell it apart from New
  Calabar; the 1884 British treaty uses "Old Calabar" for exactly that reason.
- **New Calabar is a different place.** It is Elem Kalabari, an Ijaw (Ijo) kingdom on the
  New Calabar River near Bonny, in Rivers State. It was also a slave-trading port, selling
  mainly people taken from Igboland.
- The Wikipedia article on Calabar High School says the name comes from the "Kalabari
  Kingdom." That mixes the two up. The Jamaican institutions are named after Old Calabar.
- In the voyage database, Old Calabar is place 60608 (shown as "Calabar") and New Calabar is
  place 60622.

## 2. Calabar and the slave trade to Jamaica

Portuguese sailors reached this coast in the 15th century, and "Calabar" was a European name
for the area. By the 18th century the Efik merchant houses of Duke Town and Creek Town were
selling Igbo and Ibibio captives to British ships, mostly from Bristol and Liverpool. The
Bight of Biafra sent more people to Jamaica than to anywhere else, and they were concentrated
in the north-west of the island, around Montego Bay and St Ann's Bay.

From the voyage database (imputed figures, voyages known to the database only):

| | |
|---|---|
| Voyages embarking captives at Calabar, all destinations | 955, from 1622 to 1843 |
| People embarked / disembarked on those voyages | 269,755 / 217,728 |
| Top destination region in the Caribbean | Jamaica |
| Voyages from Calabar to Jamaica | 185, from 1663 to 1807 |
| People embarked at Calabar / landed in Jamaica | 57,485 / 46,967 |
| People who died on the way from Calabar to Jamaica | 10,518 (18.3%, against 14.0% for all voyages to Jamaica) |
| Calabar's share of Bight of Biafra arrivals in Jamaica | 20.3% (Bonny: 59.4%) |
| Peak decade for arrivals from Calabar | the 1790s |

Britain abolished its slave trade in 1807, the last year of any Calabar–Jamaica voyage in the database.
Ships of other nations went on buying captives at Calabar until the early 1840s; the last
Calabar voyage in the database is from 1843, the year Calabar College opened in Jamaica. From
the 1840s Calabar's rulers turned to exporting palm oil. In 1842 King Eyamba V wrote to the
British asking for a treaty against selling people and for teachers of "book learning."

## 3. The Baptists and abolition in Jamaica (1831–1838)

- Sam Sharpe, a Baptist deacon, led the Christmas 1831 uprising, the "Baptist War." He had
  planned it as a strike.
- Planters blamed the Baptist missionaries. The Colonial Church Union burned about a dozen
  Baptist chapels, including William Knibb's at Falmouth. Knibb and Thomas Burchell were
  arrested, and Sharpe was hanged in May 1832.
- Knibb then campaigned in Britain. The uprising is widely credited with speeding up the
  Slavery Abolition Act of 1833, and full emancipation followed in 1838.

## 4. Calabar College (1843)

- In 1839, a year after emancipation, Knibb, Burchell and James Phillippo moved a resolution
  to found a college to train local pastors, missionaries and teachers. The Baptist
  Missionary Society approved it the same year, and Knibb oversaw the building.
- It opened on 3 October 1843 in "the village of Calabar," overlooking Rio Bueno in Trelawny
  (some sources say it was dedicated on 6 October). It had ten students, and its first
  president was Joshua Tinson.
- Its purpose included Africa from the start:
  - E. B. Underhill (1862) credits Knibb with the idea of Calabar as "a preparatory college
    for sending men back to Africa."
  - The 1851 Jamaica Almanac lists the "Calabar Baptist Institution for the education of young
    men for the ministry in Jamaica and Western Africa."
  - The historian G. A. Catherall adds that the main driver was probably Jamaica's own need
    for local ministers.

### Where the name came from

- The college was built in a district already called Calabar. Errol Miller, in his history of
  the Jamaica Baptist Missionary Society, writes that "some of the enslaved from Nigeria had
  brought the name of the port city on the Calabar River with them to Jamaica," and that "the
  name and location of the college were not accidental."
- So the Baptists did not invent the name. They chose a site that already bore it, at a time
  when they were planning a mission to Africa.
- **Unverified:**
  - when the Trelawny district first got the name, and who gave it. I found no record of the
    name from before 1843.
  - the Jamaica Star (2018) claim that "the Spanish named Calabar." Spain lost Jamaica in 1655,
    so this is unlikely.
  - the claim that the college was named after a British Baptist base in Calabar. The Baptist
    mission was never based there (see section 5).

## 5. The missions from Jamaica to West Africa

**The Baptist mission went to Fernando Po and Cameroon, not to Calabar.**

- 1 August 1842: the Jamaica Baptist Missionary Society is founded on the fourth anniversary
  of emancipation. Its stated objects include sending the gospel "to Africa, Central America,
  and to the Islands by which we are surrounded," and it later added support for Calabar
  College to them.
- 1840: Knibb and two Jamaican Baptists, Henry Beckford and Edward Barrett, press for a West
  African mission while in London for the first World Anti-Slavery Convention.
- 1841: John Clarke and G. K. Prince scout the coast and reach Fernando Po (now Bioko).
- About 1843–44: the *Chilmark* carries a party of Jamaicans to Fernando Po. It included
  Joseph Merrick and Joseph Jackson Fuller. Sources disagree on the date (November 1843 or
  February 1844) and on the size of the party (37 or 42).
- Merrick founds the first lasting mission on the Cameroon coast, at Bimbia. Many of the
  settlers return to Jamaica in 1847. When Spain expels the Baptists from Fernando Po in 1853,
  Alfred Saker moves the community to the mainland.

**The mission to Calabar itself was Presbyterian.**

- Hope Masterton Waddell was a Scottish Missionary Society minister who worked among enslaved
  people in Cornwall, Jamaica, from 1829, through the Baptist War.
- In 1841, at Mount Zion, he urged the Jamaica Presbytery to send a mission to West Africa.
  The Scottish church agreed in 1844, and he raised money for it in Britain.
- In 1846 he landed at Old Calabar. With him went former members of his Jamaican congregation
  who wished to "carry the gospel to their native land," and Samuel Edgerley, a printer who
  had spent eighteen years in Jamaica. He worked at Creek Town with King Eyo II.
- The Hope Waddell Training Institution in Calabar continues that line.

## 6. The name stays in Jamaica

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1868 | Calabar College moves from Rio Bueno to East Queen Street, Kingston. The Trelawny buildings are now ruins in a cattle pasture (Jamaica Star, 2018). |
| about 1904 | The college moves to Studley Park, Kingston (some sources say 1901). |
| 12 September 1912 | The Jamaica Baptist Union opens Calabar High School with 26 boys, at first for the sons of Baptist ministers. Its founders are Ernest Price, president of the college, who becomes the first headmaster, and David Davis. |
| 1919 | Elizabeth Purcell leaves the Studley Park property in trust for the school. |
| 1952 | The college and the high school move to Red Hills Road. |
| 1966 | The college merges into the United Theological College of the West Indies. |

## 7. Baptists in Nigeria: a different line

Nigerian Baptist work did not come from Jamaica.

- It began in 1850 with Thomas Jefferson Bowen, a Georgian, at Abeokuta, Ijaye and
  Ogbomosho among the Yoruba.
- Bowen was sent by the Southern Baptist Convention. That body was formed in 1845 after
  Northern Baptists refused to appoint slaveholders as missionaries.
- So Jamaican Baptism in Africa grew from the anti-slavery side of the Baptist world, and
  Nigerian Baptism from the side that defended slavery.
- The Nigerian Baptist Convention was founded in 1914.

## Open questions

- When did the Calabar district in Trelawny get its name, and who gave it? Places to look:
  the Jamaica slave registers (1817–1832), estate maps, and B. W. Higman and B. J. Hudson,
  *Jamaican Place Names*.
- Who chose the site, and do the Baptist Missionary Society papers discuss the name? Places
  to look:
  - E. B. Underhill, *The West Indies: Their Social and Religious Condition* (1862), pp. 292–3;
  - the *Missionary Herald*, 1839–1855;
  - the Baptist Missionary Society archives at the Angus Library, Regent's Park College,
    Oxford;
  - Arnold Bertram, *The History of Calabar High School: 1912–2012*.
