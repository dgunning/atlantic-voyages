# Jamaican words and their African origins

Where some everyday Jamaican words, foods and practices come from, traced to the regions of
Africa from which people were forced to Jamaica, and to the migrations that followed
emancipation.

A companion to [calabar.md](calabar.md).

## How far to trust this page

- The main reference is F. G. Cassidy and R. B. Le Page, *Dictionary of Jamaican English*
  (DJE, 1967; 2nd edition 2002). This page has **not** been checked against it. Most
  etymologies below are DJE's, but they reached this page second-hand, through Wikipedia,
  Wiktionary and dictionary sites that cite DJE page numbers.
- The one scholarly source read directly is Joseph T. Farquharson's survey "Jamaican" (2013).
  It says:
  - later research has assigned secure African etymologies to 289 Jamaican words;
  - Akan is "the chief African contributor" to Jamaican vocabulary;
  - the late seventeenth century was crucial to the language's formation.
- Every entry carries a confidence label:
  - **Well established**: standard in the dictionaries, with no serious rival.
  - **Probable**: the standard etymology, but either not confirmed here against DJE or with a
    plausible rival.
  - **Contested**: scholars disagree.
  - **Unverified**: found only in weak sources.
- A shared word does not prove a single source. Many West African languages have similar
  words, and Jamaican speech formed from many languages meeting on the plantations.

## When people arrived

Shares of people landed in Jamaica, by African region of embarkation and period. These come
from the voyage database's imputed figures, for voyages whose region is known; about a quarter
of arrivals have no known region. The figures are from the query at the end of this page, not
from a checked notebook.

| Period | People landed (region known) | Gold Coast | Bight of Biafra | West Central Africa | Bight of Benin | Senegambia |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| to 1700 | 46,052 | 8.9% | 19.6% | 29.6% | 34.1% | 6.2% |
| 1701–1750 | 141,995 | 37.8% | 17.3% | 19.0% | 19.0% | 4.1% |
| 1751–1775 | 184,062 | 33.2% | 29.6% | 9.6% | 10.3% | 2.1% |
| 1776–1790 | 119,092 | 40.5% | 36.9% | 4.0% | 9.9% | 1.8% |
| 1791–1807 | 215,526 | 15.4% | 45.8% | 27.2% | 4.1% | 0.5% |

Farquharson's own table for 1655–1700, from an earlier edition of the same database, shows
the same pattern. The early population came mainly from the Bight of Benin (Gbe and Yoruba
speakers) and West Central Africa (Kongo and Mbundu). Akan speakers from the Gold Coast
dominated the 1700s, and Igbo, Efik and Ibibio speakers from the Bight of Biafra came mostly
at the end of the trade.

That timing matters for etymologies. The language was taking shape in the late 1600s, so a
word from a late-arriving group either entered later or came through a group that was already
there.

Britain ended its slave trade in 1807 and slavery itself in 1834–1838. New migrations then
followed (section 7).

## 1. Gold Coast: Akan (Twi, Fante, Asante)

Peak arrivals: 1701–1790.

| Jamaican | Meaning | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quashie, Cudjoe, Cubbena, Quaco, Quao, Cuffee, Quamin | Men's names | Akan day names: Kwasi (Sunday), Kwadwo/Kojo (Monday), Kwabena (Tuesday), Kwaku (Wednesday), Yaw (Thursday), Kofi (Friday), Kwame (Saturday) | Well established |
| Quasheba, Juba, Cooba, Abba, Phibba, Mimba | Women's names | The female day names: Akosua, Adwoa, Akua, Yaa, Afua, Ama | Well established |
| Anansi, Anancy | Spider trickster of the folktales | Twi *ananse*, "spider" | Well established |
| Coromantee | Planters' label for captives from the Gold Coast | Kormantse (Cormantin), an English fort on the Fante coast | Well established |
| Kromanti | Maroon ritual language and ceremony (Kromanti Play) | Largely Akan vocabulary (Bilby 1983) | Well established |
| ackee | The fruit, now the national fruit | Twi *ankye*. The plant is West African; Dr Thomas Clarke planted it in eastern Jamaica in 1778. | Probable |
| duckunoo, dokunoo (also "blue drawers", "tie-a-leaf") | Pudding of grated green banana, sweet potato or cornmeal, with coconut and spice, boiled in a leaf | Fante/Twi *dɔkono*, a boiled maize dough (kenkey). The Jamaican dish is an adaptation, not the same recipe. | Probable (DJE p. 155, via Wikipedia) |
| fufu, foofoo | Pounded yam, plantain or cassava | Twi *fufuo*, "white"; Ewe has the same word | Probable |
| kas-kas | Quarrel, strife | Twi *kasakasa*, "to dispute" | Probable |

Notes:
- **Quashie** became a dismissive generic name for a Black person in British writing of the
  1800s (Carlyle used it in 1853). In Jamaica today it means someone of no account. No
  scholarly history of how the meaning shifted was found.
- **Tacky's War** (1760, St Mary) was a Coromantee uprising. Its leader's name is Akan
  (Takyi). See Vincent Brown, *Tacky's Revolt* (2020).
- **Nanny** and **Cudjoe** of the Maroons bear Akan names. Nanny's Asante origin is Maroon
  tradition; the written record of her life is thin.

## 2. Bight of Biafra: Igbo, Efik, Ibibio

Peak arrivals: 1751–1807. Most came through Bonny and Old Calabar (see [calabar.md](calabar.md)).

| Jamaican | Meaning | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **backra, buckra** | White person; boss, master | Efik and Ibibio *mbakára*, "white man, European" | Probable to well established |
| unu, unnu | You (plural) | Igbo *ùnù*, "you (plural)" | Probable (DJE p. 457, via Wikipedia) |
| okra | The vegetable | Igbo *ọkwụrụ* | Probable |
| Eboe, Ibo | Planters' label for Igbo people | Igbo *Ìgbò* | Well established |
| red Eboe | Light-skinned person | Compound of "red" and Eboe | Probable |
| Moco, Mocco | Planters' label | Probably Ibibio and other Cross River peoples behind Old Calabar; the exact scope is disputed | Contested |

Notes:
- **backra** is the clearest language link to Calabar. It is the dictionaries' standard
  etymology (Cassidy; McWhorter 2000). The linguist Salikoko Mufwene doubts it is purely
  African, because there is no sign of enough Efik speakers early in the period. "Back raw"
  (from floggings) is a folk etymology.
- A planned Igbo uprising in St Elizabeth in 1815–16 is reported, with a "King of the Eboes."
  The sources disagree on the year and the numbers, and the evidence came largely from
  interrogation under torture.
- Abakuá, the Efik-based Ekpe society, was recreated in **Cuba**, not Jamaica. No Jamaican
  Ekpe society is documented.

## 3. West Central Africa: Kongo, Mbundu

Strong before 1700 and again in 1791–1807. A second wave came after emancipation (section 7).

| Jamaican | Meaning | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| pinda | Peanut | Kikongo *mpinda* | Well established |
| Kumina | A Kongo-based religion and drumming tradition, centred on St Thomas | Kongo (Bilby and Fu-Kiau 1983). Its ritual language mixes Kikongo and Jamaican. | Well established |
| dundus | Person with albinism | Kikongo *ndundu* | Probable (DJE p. 112, via Wikipedia) |
| gungo peas | Pigeon peas | "Congo pea", a label for the plant's African association, not a Kikongo word | Probable |
| kata | Head pad for carrying loads | Kikongo *nkata*, "porter's pad", or Twi *kata*, "to cover" | Contested |
| callaloo | Leafy greens; the dish | Kimbundu *kalulú*, or Tupi *caárurú*, "thick leaf" | Contested |

## 6. Words whose origin is argued over

- **obeah** (spiritual practice; in colonial law, a crime):
  - Igbo *dibịa* or *ọbịa*, "healer, doctoring", with Efik and Ibibio relatives. Handler and
    Bilby (2001) note that the word is first recorded where Biafran captives were numerous.
  - Akan *ɔbayifo*, "witch", the older view (Williams 1933, Patterson).
  - Efik *ubio*, a charm buried to cause harm (cited by the OED).
- **duppy** (ghost):
  - Akan or Ga *adópe*, "dwarf, spirit" (DJE p. 4; Allsopp).
  - Bube *dupe*, "ghost". Bube is the language of Bioko (Fernando Po), where the Jamaican
    Baptist mission landed in the 1840s ([calabar.md](calabar.md)).
  - Origin unsettled.
- **Jonkonnu, John Canoe** (Christmas masquerade):
  - John Conny, an Ahanta (Akan) trader at Axim in the 1720s. This is the most cited theory,
    going back to Edward Long (1774).
  - Igbo or Efik masquerades.
  - European folk etymologies such as "gens inconnus" ("unknown people").
  - The masquerade itself mixes West African and British mumming traditions.

## 7. After emancipation: new migrations

| Arrivals | Who | Traces |
|---|---|---|
| 1834–1835 | German settlers, through Bremen; about 250–300 to Seaford Town, Westmoreland, in December 1835 | Seaford Town |
| 1841–1865 | "Liberated Africans": people freed by the Royal Navy from intercepted slave ships, landed at Sierra Leone or St Helena, then brought to Jamaica as indentured labourers. Schuler (1980) counts about 8,000; other counts exceed 10,000. Mainly Kongo and other Central Africans, and Yoruba, with some Igbo, Kalabari and Mende. | Kumina in St Thomas; Etu in Hanover; the Yoruba village of Abeokuta in Westmoreland |
| 1845–1910s | Indian indentured labourers, mostly from north India; about 36,000. The first ship arrived on 10 May 1845; sources give 1912–1917 for the end of the scheme. | **ganja** (Hindi *gāñjā*, "hemp"); curry; the **Hosay** festival (Muharram), now best known in Clarendon |
| from 1854 | Chinese migrants, mostly Hakka from Guangdong | The rural shop, the "Chiney shop" |

## 8. From neither Africa nor Asia

| Jamaican | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| pickney (child) | Portuguese *pequenino*, "very small", by way of the Atlantic pidgins (compare "pickaninny") | Probable |
| jerk | Spanish *charqui*, from Quechua *ch'arki*, "dried meat"; Hans Sloane records "jirking" in 1687 | Well established for the word. The cooking method is credited to the Taíno and the Maroons, which is probable but weakly sourced. |
| bammy | A cassava flatbread descended from Taíno *casabe* | Well established for the food; the word's origin is unknown |
| patty | Pastry turnover like the Cornish pasty, with spices and curry added after Indian arrival | Probable; popular sources only |

## Left out on purpose

- **Eboe Town** as a Jamaican place name: no source found.
- "Big eye", "red eye" and "bad mind" as translations of Igbo phrases: no scholarly support
  found.
- Adinkra symbols in Jamaica: no attestation found.

## Reproducing the arrivals table

```python
import pandas as pd
from voyages import Voyages

v = Voyages()
d = v.transatlantic.disembarked_in("Jamaica").to_pandas(
    ["embarkation_region_name", "year_arrived_imputed", "disembarked_imputed"]
)
d = d[d.embarkation_region_name != "Other Africa"].dropna()
d["period"] = pd.cut(
    d.year_arrived_imputed,
    [1600, 1700, 1750, 1775, 1790, 1808],
    labels=["to 1700", "1701-1750", "1751-1775", "1776-1790", "1791-1807"],
)
t = d.pivot_table(
    index="period",
    columns="embarkation_region_name",
    values="disembarked_imputed",
    aggfunc="sum",
    observed=False,
)
(t.div(t.sum(axis=1), axis=0) * 100).round(1)
```
