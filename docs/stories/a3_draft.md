**Standfirst.** For 45 years, slaving ships brought more than 40,000 African captives into
Montego Bay. Most came from one stretch of coast, around the Niger delta and the Cross River,
far more than at Kingston. Twenty-three years after the last ship, the parishes they served
rose in the Christmas Rebellion.

On 18 January 1793 the *African Queen*, a Bristol ship of 277 tons, came into Montego Bay
from Old Calabar, on the Cross River in what is now south-eastern Nigeria. She had left
Bristol a year and two days earlier [F20]. When she left the African coast there were 330
people aboard. At Montego Bay, 214 were landed [F13]. The ship's record does not say what
became of the other 116.

The record says a little more. Of the 255 people whose age and sex were noted when they were
taken aboard, 138 were women, 93 were men, 14 were girls and 10 were boys [F14]. The crew fell
from 37 to 18 during the voyage, and the record names three captains, one after another:
Samuel Stribling, Hamet Forsyth and James Lloyd [F20]. **[REVIEW: is it fair to say the
change of captains suggests deaths among the officers, or leave it unexplained, as here?]**

We do not know the names of the 214 people, or which estates bought them. What we can know
is that they were part of a pattern, and that the pattern shaped the western parishes.

### Jamaica's second port

Kingston was Jamaica's great slave market. But slaving ships also sailed direct from Africa
to a second port on the north coast: Montego Bay, the harbour for St James and the parishes
around it.

The SlaveVoyages database, compiled by historians from shipping and port records in archives
on both sides of the Atlantic, records 144 slaving voyages that landed captives at Montego
Bay. The first in the database arrived in 1763 and the last in 1808 [F1, F4]. On the 133 voyages whose records give a
count, 40,050 people were landed [F2]. Because some voyages have no count, the true number
was higher.

The trade peaked in the 1790s, with 70 voyages and 20,574 people landed in that decade alone
[F5]. In 1792, 21 ships landed captives at Montego Bay [F6]. Most of the ships came from
Liverpool (88 of the 142 voyages with a recorded year), then Bristol (28) and London (18)
[F11].

### One coast

What sets Montego Bay apart is where its captives came from.

Of the people landed there whose African region is recorded, 71% had been bought in the
Bight of Biafra [F7]. At Kingston over the same years, the figure was 36% [F8]. Kingston drew
on many coasts: the Gold Coast (today's Ghana), the Bight of Benin, and West Central Africa
(the Loango coast, Kongo and Angola), which supplied 22% of the people landed at Kingston.
From West Central Africa, Montego Bay received no one [F9].

Within the Bight of Biafra, the Montego Bay ships loaded almost entirely at three ports in
what is now Nigeria: Bonny, New Calabar and Old Calabar [F10, F17]. These were the outlets
for Igbo country and for the Ibibio and other peoples of the Cross River. **[REVIEW: the
Igbo and Ibibio description of who was sold through Bonny, New Calabar and Old Calabar.]**

### The people who stayed

Where a ship landed its captives is not where they lived. Planters from across the island
bought at Kingston, and some people landed in Jamaica were shipped on to Spanish America. So
is there evidence from Jamaica itself?

There is, in the newspapers. When an enslaved person escaped, the person claiming them often
paid for a notice describing them, and many notices named an African "country". On 19 May
1784, the Montego Bay paper, the *Cornwall Chronicle*, carried a notice for three men,
Billy, Quashie and Dick, "all of the Eboe country". **[CHECK: quotation against the
original notice in Chambers's transcriptions.]**

Historian Douglas Chambers transcribed these notices, and Anthony Wood and Billy Smith turned
them into a dataset, published by Enslaved.org. Among the African-born runaways advertised in
the *Cornwall Chronicle* between 1775 and 1795 whose "country" can be matched to a region,
37% were described with terms from the Bight of Biafra, such as Eboe, Igbo or Moco. In the
Kingston papers it was 17% [F15]. The numbers are small (216 people in the Montego Bay paper,
77 in Kingston's), but the difference is larger than chance would explain [F19].

These are enslavers' words, not the people's own. "Eboe" could mean a birthplace or a
cultural label. But the notices point the same way as the ships.

They also show that the ships are not the whole story. About a fifth of the African-born
runaways in the Montego Bay paper were described with terms from West Central Africa, such as
Congo, although no ship landed anyone from there at Montego Bay [F16]. They had been bought
elsewhere, most likely at Kingston, and taken west. **[REVIEW: "most likely at Kingston".]**

### The last ship

In 1807 Parliament abolished the British slave trade. Ships could no longer clear from
British ports for Africa after 1 May 1807, and no captives could be landed in the colonies
after 1 March 1808. **[CHECK: dates against the text of the Act.]** The last voyage to land
captives at Montego Bay in the database is the *Ruby* of Liverpool, in 1808, with 161 people
from the Bight of Biafra [F12].

### Kensington, 1831

Twenty-three years later, on 27 December 1831, fire broke out at Kensington estate, in the
hills above Montego Bay. It was the start of the largest rising of enslaved people in the
history of the British Caribbean, known as the Christmas Rebellion or the Baptist War.
**[CHECK: "largest in the British Caribbean"; and 27 vs 28 December for the fire.]** It
had been planned by Samuel Sharpe, a Baptist deacon, as a strike for wages after Christmas;
when the planters refused, it became an armed rising across the western parishes. Sharpe was
hanged in Montego Bay on 23 May 1832. **[CHECK: place of execution.]** **[REVIEW: number
killed and executed, with one named source; sources differ.]**

The voyage data cannot say who rose in 1831. By then many enslaved people in the west had
been born in Jamaica, Sharpe among them. **[CHECK: Sharpe's birthplace.]** What the data
shows is who had been brought to Montego Bay in the forty-five years before abolition: most
of them were people from the Bight of Biafra. They, and their children, were part of the
population that rose. **[REVIEW: the African-born share of St James's enslaved population in the
1817–1832 registers, if known; this paragraph should stay this cautious without it.]**

### What we still do not know

The landing port is the closest these records come to where people lived. The slave
registers of 1817 to 1832, which list enslaved people estate by estate and often note whether
each was African-born, could show where Africans from each coast actually lived, parish by
parish. They are not yet open as data. **[REVIEW: any published parish-level analysis of
the registers.]**

The 214 people landed from the *African Queen* in January 1793 are in no register we can
search. But we know the coast they came from, the port they reached and the year they
arrived. In St James, that was a common story.
